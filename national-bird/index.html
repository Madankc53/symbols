National Bird of Nepal

Danphe — Himalayan Monal

Nepal's national bird is the Danphe, or Himalayan Monal (Lophophorus impejanus) — a Himalayan pheasant whose male plumage shifts through metallic green, blue, copper and violet in changing light.

Scientific name: Lophophorus impejanus Family: Phasianidae Elevation: ~2,500–4,750 m IUCN status: Least Concern

Walk high enough into Nepal's Himalayan forests in spring, and you may catch a flash of metallic green and copper darting across a grassy slope. That flash is the Danphe — a bird admired across the country for its beauty, symbolism and dramatic seasonal movements.

Appearance: A Bird of Two Very Different Colours

The Himalayan Monal shows one of the most dramatic examples of sexual dimorphism among Himalayan birds — the male and female look almost like different species entirely.

Male: Iridescent metallic green crest, coppery back and neck, blue-purple wings, and a prominent white rump most visible in flight. Colours visibly shift depending on the angle of light.

Female: Mottled brown plumage with a white throat patch — muted colouring that camouflages her while nesting and caring for chicks on the forest floor.

The male's crest and colours are so prized that poaching for feathers, historically used as symbols of authority, remains one of the species' biggest threats.

Source: Department of National Parks and Wildlife Conservation, Nepal

Habitat and Seasonal Movement

In summer the Danphe lives on steep grassy and rocky slopes and nearby forest in the alpine and sub-alpine zone, roughly 3,300 to 4,750 metres, according to Nepal's Department of National Parks and Wildlife Conservation. In winter it moves down into rhododendron forest, to about 2,500 metres.

In winter, deep snow pushes the birds down into lower rhododendron and conifer forest; by summer they return to open grassy slopes and cliffs near the tree line to breed.

Diet and Behaviour

Danphe are strong diggers, using their strong bills to dig into soil and snow in search of tubers, roots, seeds, berries, mosses and insects. They are largely ground-dwelling and can be surprisingly bold when feeding.

Culture and Symbolism

The Danphe holds a warm place in Nepali culture — it appears frequently in Nepali folk songs, traditional art and provincial imagery, and sighting one in the wild is often regarded as a sign of both beauty and good fortune.

Regional neighbours: the Himalayan Monal is also the state bird of Uttarakhand, India.

Conservation Status

The Himalayan Monal is listed as Least Concern on the global IUCN Red List, meaning it isn't considered at imminent risk of extinction — but its population trend is decreasing due to poaching, habitat loss, and disturbance near human settlements. Nepal's national assessment rates it Near Threatened, and the species is fully protected (Appendix I) under the National Parks and Wildlife Conservation Act 1973.

Source: IUCN Red List; Department of National Parks and Wildlife Conservation, Nepal

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the national bird of Nepal?

The Danphe, also called the Himalayan Monal (Lophophorus impejanus), is the national bird of Nepal.

Why is the Danphe important in Nepal?

The Danphe is admired for its iridescent plumage and is considered a symbol of peace and natural beauty in Nepal, often referenced in folk songs and traditional art.

What does a Himalayan Monal look like?

The male has iridescent metallic green, blue, copper and purple plumage with a prominent white rump, while the female is mottled brown for camouflage while nesting.

Where does the Danphe live in Nepal?

It lives in Himalayan oak-conifer forests and alpine meadows, in summer mostly between about 3,300 and 4,750 metres, descending to roughly 2,500 metres in winter, according to Nepal's Department of National Parks and Wildlife Conservation.

Is the national bird of Nepal endangered?

Globally the Himalayan Monal is listed as Least Concern by the IUCN, though its population is decreasing due to poaching and habitat loss, it is rated Near Threatened within Nepal, and it is fully protected under Nepal's National Parks and Wildlife Conservation Act 1973.
